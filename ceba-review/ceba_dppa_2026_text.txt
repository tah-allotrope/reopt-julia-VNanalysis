Module 2 - The Five-Line Bill
The Monthly DPPA Settlement Has Five Lines
# | Line | Formula | Level (2025 reference)
1 | Market energy | Q_Khc x FMP x k x K_pp | FMP avg ~1,426.6 VND/kWh; k~1.026; K_pp by voltage
2 | DPPA service fee | Q_Khc x C_dppa_dv | 360 VND/kWh
3 | Balancing fee | Q_Khc x P_cl | 163.3 VND/kWh
4 | Residual purchase | (Load - Q_Khc) x EVN retail | TOU rates
5 | CfD settlement | (Strike - FMP) x min(Q_c, Q_Khc) | Two-way: can be + or -
Lines 1-4 are billed by EVN. Line 5 settles bilaterally with the generator under CfD contract.
Q_Khc = the renewable volume actually received and consumed (defined next slide).
CFMP = FMP x k: k converts the market price to the customer delivery point (price conversion, not a volume loss).

[Slide 10]
Module 2 - The Five-Line Bill
Contracted Volumes & Actual Electricity Consumption
Q_adj = Q_meter / K_pp        delivered volume at your meter
Q_Khc = min(Load, Q_adj)      settled volume (lines 1-3)
Q_CfD = min(Q_c, Q_Khc)       CfD settles on received volume
Grid losses are reflected through coordinated volume and price conversions: K_pp converts generator-meter output into buyer-side credited volume, while k and K_pp convert FMP into the corresponding buyer-side market-energy price.
If the plant generates more than you consume in an hour, the excess earns the generator spot revenue only; it is NOT billed to you and does NOT earn CfD
Practical consequence: Contracted volume (Q_c) above your real consumption buys nothing
Which volume? You settle only on what you actually consume

CfD Mechanics: What Drives Your Quoted Price

[Slide 15]
Module 3 - CfD Mechanics
The CfD Is Two-Way: Certainty, Not Automatic Savings
CfD cash flow = (Strike - FMP) x Q_CfD
Market condition | Who pays whom
FMP below strike | You top up the generator
FMP above strike | Generator pays you back
Net effect: the renewable energy cost is locked near the strike (+ fees), whatever the market does
2025 reference: FMP averaged ~1,427 VND/kWh with strong hourly volatility; the CfD removes that volatility from the budget
A strike below expected average FMP would mean expected receipts, but no developer can finance that (Module 4)

[Slide 16]
Module 3 - CfD Mechanics
Strike Escalation & The Year 1 Reality
Strike escalation (e.g., 4%/yr)
Escalating strikes improve the seller's and lender's lifetime economics...
...but do nothing for the Year 1 cost and compound against BUYER later
Negotiate the escalation index as hard as the starting strike (fixed VND vs CPI vs USD-linked)
What Year 1 usually looks like
In most realistic structures, Year 1 DPPA cost is at or above BAU
Savings build as EVN tariffs escalate (~4%/yr) against BUYER locked strike
→ Advice: Evaluate on 10-year and lifetime cumulative cost, not on Year 1 alone
Internal Budget Question to Consider: Can we accept a modest Year 1 premium to cap 15~25 year exposure?

[Slide 17]
Module 3 - CfD Mechanics
Contract Volume VS Your Load Shape
Solar generation peaks  midday, but the factory runs its own shape. The overlap—hour by hour - is what matters most.
If you... | Then… | Cost consequence
Over-contract  (Q_c > consumption) | CfD caps at consumed volume - excess earns nothing | You paid contract terms for volume you can’t  settle
Match contract to hourly overlap | Every contracted kWh settles | Efficient; this is the target
Under-contract  (Q_c < overlap) | More residual purchased from EVN at TOU | Less price certainty, smaller renewable claim
Ask every developer for an hourly (8760 hour) matching analysis against your meter data, not an annual percentage
Batteries can shift the overlap window but add CAPEX the tariff must carry (see the case study)

[Slide 20]
Module 4 - Developer Economics
A Deal Must Pass Three Gates to Proceed
Gate | Who | Test | Strike pressure
1. Buyer | You | Cumulative cost <= BAU over 10 yr AND lifetime | Pushes strike DOWN
2. Seller | Developer | Equity IRR >= 12-15% | Pushes strike UP
3. Lender | Bank | Minimum DSCR >= ~1.20x every year | Pushes strike UP (hardest gate)
DSCR = 					, computed for every year of the loan
One CAPEX heavy year ( e.g., a battery replacement) can sink the whole financing even if averages look fine
The negotiation window is the strike range where all three gates “pass”; but  it can be empty (see case study)
A strike below the developer's bankability floor does not mean a cheap deal; 
it means no project.

Module 4 - Developer Economics
Key Lifecycle Items The Tariff Must Contain
Item | Typical assumption | Buyer Implication
PV degradation | ~0.5%/yr output decline | Matched volume shrinks; lost kWh repurchased from EVN at TOU
Battery replacement | Large capex every 8-10 years | Can highly impact DSCR in that replacement year

These rarely appear in headline term sheets, but each one moves the bankable strike floor and therefore your price.

[Slide 27]
Module 5 - Case StudyWhat This Means And Expectations
Expect low single-digit lifetime savings at best. The product you are buying is 10-25 year price certainty, meet up to 100% of annual electricity demand with contracted renewable generation, and utilize credible renewable claims, but all at a price premium.
Expect Year 1 to cost more than BAU. Plan the internal approval around cumulative horizons.
Strike offers near 2,000 VND/kWh leave the buyer deeply negative. Realistic buyer-positive territory starts near ~1,300 VND, which today fails the lender.
Deal feasibility is decided by financing structure as much as by price. Ask developers HOW their project is financed, not just what they charge.

Module 6 - Decision Points
Five Questions That Change The Term Sheet
# | Decision | What to check
1 | Strike level & escalation/indexation | Fixed VND vs escalating/deescalating vs USD linked; model these vs BAU
2 | Contract volume vs load shape | 8760 hourly matching vs meter data; CfD caps at consumed volume
3 | Tenor & termination | Developer wants 15-25 yr; match to your lease / order book; exit terms
4 | Voltage eligibility & K_pp | >=22kV only today; which K_pp applies decides your loss adjustment
5 | Risk allocation | FMP volatility, curtailment, negative CfD months, change-in-law
Items 1-4 are quantifiable before negotiation; run them against load data first.

Scenario 1: The Base Case
Your manufacturing plant has a strict corporate target to reach 100% renewable energy consumption by 2030. To achieve this, your team wants to sign a DPPA via National Grid (Virtual DPPA) with a large solar developer.
Operational issue: Your factory doesn’t buy electricity directly from EVN. Instead, you pay your monthly electricity bill to the private Industrial Park Management Board, who buys bulk power from EVN and resells it to you through their internal grid.
Questions:
Under Decree 57, an Electricity Buyer must consume ≥200,000 kWh/month. If the IP lumps your factory into their master EVN meter, how do you accurately track and prove your specific energy use to qualify?
A standard Virtual PPA is a bilateral agreement (Between Buyer and Seller). However, since electricity delivery is controlled by the IP (Retailer), who actually needs to be at the negotiation table to make this work?
What happens if the IP demands a new, non-negotiable "infrastructure fee" to facilitate this DPPA setup? How does that impact your budget forecasting and management buy-in over a typical 15-to-20-year PPA Contract Term?

CALCULATIONS
Cost paid to EVN (for 900,000 kWh of renewables + 100,000 kWh additional):
Market electricity energy cost = 900,000 * 1,600 * 1.026 * 1.008 = 1,489,259,520 VND
DPPA service fee: 900,000 * 360 = 324,000,000 VND
Difference clearing fee: 900,000 * 163.30 = 146,970,000 VND
Additional electricity purchase: (1,000,000 - 900,000) * 1,800 = 180,000,000 VND (buy 100,000 kWh from EVN at ~1,800 VND/kWh)
→ Total cost collected by EVN: C_EVN = 1,489,259,520 + 324,000,000 + 146,970,000 + 180,000,000 = 2,140,229,520 VND
CfD contract cost (calculated separately for each plant):  
For plant X: (P_cX - SMP) * Q_cX = (1,500 - 1,600) * 600,000 = -60,000,000 VND  
For plant Y: (P_cY - SMP)* Q_cY = (1,700 - 1,600) * 300,000 = +30,000,000 VND  
Total CfD cost (net): -60,000,000 + 30,000,000 = -30,000,000 VND.  
Total cost of customer C: C_KH = C_EVN + C_CfD = 2,140,229,520 + (-30,000,000) = 2,110,229,520 VND

Recap and Wrap Up

[Slide 55]
Corporate demand for RE in Vietnam is growing: The C&I sector has the potential to send strong demand signals for accessible, expanded, cost-effective RE in Vietnam.
Start with your load and emissions data. Clean energy procurement decisions should begin with goal setting, facility-level electricity consumption, load profile, Scope 1–2 emissions, and any relevant Scope 3 or product carbon footprint requirements.
Match the solution to the facility constraint. On-site solar is the most available near-term option, but roof space, facility ownership, tenancy terms, and industrial park rules constraints will determine feasibility.
Treat BESS as a procurement design tool, not only a technology add-on. Battery storage can improve tariff resilience, increase daily renewable energy consumption, support peak shaving, and improve the commercial case for solar under evolving tariff structures.
Use RECs/EACs carefully and transparently. Unbundled RECs/EACs may support Scope 2 claims, but buyers should understand attribute ownership, additionality concerns, and the use cases between EACs and carbon offsets.
Monitor DPPA developments, but plan for uncertainty. DPPA may unlock larger off-site procurement, but buyers should account for evolving cost inputs, wheeling charges, settlement structures, and first-mover implementation challenges.
Challenges persist: While on-site clean energy procurement options are available today in Vietnam, scalability and further replication is limited by evolving policy, market, and technical challenges.
